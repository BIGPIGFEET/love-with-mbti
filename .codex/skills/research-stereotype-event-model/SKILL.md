# Research Stereotype Event Model

Turn online stereotype discourse into clearly bounded narrative-design evidence. Treat it as a study of how a group is represented online, never as a diagnosis or a claim about every real person.

## Workflow

1. Set the research boundary: target label/community, geographic/language coverage, intended creative output, and whether the user needs representative statistics or only recurring online narratives. State that content frequency is not prevalence.
2. Build a source plan before searching. Cover at least three independent source clusters when feasible: primary community self-reports, public social/video platforms, and explanatory or official/type-reference pages. Separate original posts from reposts and AI-generated summaries.
3. Search in rounds. Start broad; use the first results to form precise behavior, relationship, conflict, and counter-stereotype queries. Keep a query ledger for each platform. Respect logins, paywalls, and robots restrictions; do not claim coverage where access failed.
4. Record only inspectable evidence: URL, page/title, platform, date when available, content type, summary, and the narrow claim it supports. Retain counterexamples and explicit critiques of the stereotype.
5. Synthesize into a neutral feature-evidence table. Give every feature a scope, a source set, a confidence/frequency label describing online discourse, and a creative-use restriction. Do not turn self-selected polls into population percentages.
6. Convert only well-supported, narratively useful features into a behavior model. Express each as an observable tendency with a constructive potential and a situational risk—not a moral trait or personality certainty.
7. Derive event material by pairing competing but legitimate needs. Map each candidate scene to a relationship stage, selected features, conflict dimension, affected state variables, and design guardrails.
8. Audit the result. Remove duplicates, check phase progression, ensure player choices have agency, and ensure a label never becomes an excuse for harm, control, unreliability, or lack of accountability.

## Evidence Rules

- Prefer direct pages and primary self-reports. Treat search snippets as leads until the original page is inspectable.
- Use labels such as `cross-platform recurring`, `repeated`, `context-specific`, and `low-confidence meme`; these classify discourse evidence, not real-world frequency.
- State platform failures explicitly in both the source table and limitations section.
- Preserve disagreement: a negative trope and its critique can both be findings about online representation.
- Cite sources beside research claims. Separate sourced observations from creative inference.

## Required Deliverables

Create the following, adapting names and format to the project:

- Source and query ledger, including inaccessible platforms and fallback decisions.
- Feature-evidence table with neutral phrasing, support, confidence, risks, and permitted creative use.
- Behavior model grouped by relationship stage (or another explicit progression).
- Conflict dictionary with both parties' legitimate needs.
- Candidate-event table with complete stage/feature/conflict/state mapping.
- Limitations note distinguishing online narratives from empirical population claims.

Use the column schemas and checks in [references/table-schemas.md](references/table-schemas.md). Keep raw research, the behavior model, and scene/event catalogues in separate files once the material grows beyond a single short document.

## Creative Safety Boundaries

- Do not diagnose individuals or represent stereotypes as facts about a protected, clinical, or demographic group.
- Attribute harmful choices to specific actions and consequences, not to the label.
- Give every conflict more than one valid response. Avoid an always-correct "please the stereotype" route.
- Make relationship variables observable (for example: attraction, trust, comfort, conflict) and do not let frequent messaging automatically equal intimacy or consent.
